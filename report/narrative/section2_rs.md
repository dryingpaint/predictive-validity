Rare-variant and Mendelian evidence ({{section2.rs.genetic_rare.rs|x2}}×) and GWAS evidence ({{section2.rs.genetic_gwas.rs|x2}}×) both carry the genetic signal, in line with Minikel et al. (2024), who found 2.6× from Phase 1 to launch with Mendelian evidence stronger than GWAS. Somatic driver evidence ({{section2.rs.somatic.rs|x2}}×), mouse phenotypes ({{section2.rs.animal_model.rs|x2}}×) and Reactome pathways ({{section2.rs.affected_pathway.rs|x2}}×, from only {{section2.rs.affected_pathway.n_with|int}} programs) also associate with success; differential expression in the disease does not clearly ({{section2.rs.rna_expression.rs|x2}}×, interval spanning 1). Loss-of-function-intolerant targets show no penalty ({{section2.rs.constrained.rs|x2}}×), while pan-essential targets reach approval less than a third as often ({{section2.rs.pan_essential.rs|x2}}×), mostly oncology small molecules aimed at machinery every cell needs (mTOR, tubulin, topoisomerases, histone deacetylases, WEE1 and ATR, BET proteins). CRISPR-screen evidence covered too few programs ({{section2.rs.functional_screen.n_with|int}}) to estimate.

Two checks bear on whether this reflects foresight rather than hindsight. Evidence is taken as it stood in Open Targets' yearly series before the program entered the phase, so later discoveries do not count. Counting all evidence known by 2026 instead weakens the genetic relative success to {{section2.rs_untimed.genetic_any_untimed.rs|x2}}×: what was known in advance is more telling than what was found afterwards.
