## Evolving diseases: infection and cancer

Infection and cancer are left out of the per-disease labels because their combinations mostly exist to stay ahead of evolution, not because one patient's biology needs several targets. This section uses the same words: **solved by one drug** (one molecule acting through at most three pathways), **solved by stacking**, or **unsolved**. Every figure below comes from the cited trial or cohort paper.

**In short**

* **Evolving pathogens are solved by stacking from the start.** One pill holding two antivirals clears hepatitis C in 99% of patients, three drugs hold HIV down for life, and three or four drugs treat drug-resistant tuberculosis successfully in about nine in ten patients. Each drug alone selected resistant organisms within weeks to months.
* **In infection the combination blocks escape.** A pathogen population is large enough to carry a mutant resistant to any one drug before treatment starts, but very unlikely to carry one resistant to all of them.
* **In cancer one targeted drug gives durable control in only a few driver-defined diseases:** CML on imatinib, and now ALK-driven lung cancer on lorlatinib, with CLL on continuous BTK inhibition close behind. EGFR-driven lung cancer, BRAF-mutant melanoma and metastatic HER2-positive breast cancer still progress within a few years in most patients.
* **Up-front combinations help cancer by months, not cures,** and most approved cancer combinations work because different patients respond to different drugs, not because the drugs add up inside one patient. Immunotherapy gives a durable tail to a minority. A personalised neoantigen vaccine has one positive Phase 3 trial, in melanoma after surgery, with two years of follow-up.

### Infectious disease: stacking from the start

**Hepatitis C: solved by stacking, in one pill.** Sofosbuvir (an NS5B polymerase inhibitor) and velpatasvir (an NS5A inhibitor), taken as one tablet for 12 weeks, cleared the virus in 99% of 624 patients with genotypes 1, 2, 4, 5 or 6, including those with cirrhosis or earlier treatment failure; none of 116 on placebo cleared it ([Feld 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26571066/)). A single antiviral was not enough: in 14 days of the protease inhibitor telaprevir alone, resistant virus was selected rapidly, and the investigators concluded that combining it with interferon or other antivirals seemed mandatory ([Sarrazin 2007](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17484874/)).

**HIV: solved by stacking, as lifelong control rather than cure.** Zidovudine alone selected less sensitive virus: most patients treated for six months or more carried it, and 5 of 15 carried virus with a 100-fold loss of sensitivity ([Larder 1989](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/2467383/)). HIV turns over through so many replication cycles in one person that mutations conferring drug resistance accumulate predictably and are likely present before therapy begins ([Coffin 1995](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/7824947/)). Adding a third drug, indinavir, to two nucleoside analogues halved progression to AIDS or death (6% vs 11%; hazard ratio 0.50) ([Hammer 1997](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9287227/)). Today a 40-year-old on long-term combination therapy with a high CD4 count can expect to live only a few years less than the general population ([Trickey 2023](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36958365/)); in a US health system, the life-expectancy gap at age 20 fell from 44 years in 1996–1997 to 12 years in 2011, and to 8 years for people who started treatment early ([Marcus 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27028501/)).

**Tuberculosis: solved by stacking, since 1950.** In the first Medical Research Council trials, streptomycin alone produced resistance in 70% of patients after 120 days; adding para-aminosalicylic acid cut that to at most 9%. Isoniazid alone produced resistance in 70% after three months. The three-drug regimen worked because resistance to all three at once would need about 1 in 10<sup>15</sup> bacilli, while a severe cavitary infection holds 10<sup>8</sup> to 10<sup>10</sup> ([Kerantzas 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28292983/)). The same logic now treats drug-resistant disease. Bedaquiline, pretomanid and linezolid (BPaL), three drugs with little pre-existing resistance, for 26 weeks gave a favourable outcome in 90% of 109 patients with extensively drug-resistant or treatment-failing tuberculosis ([Conradie 2020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32130813/)) and in 84–93% across four linezolid doses in a randomised follow-up ([Conradie 2022](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36053506/)). Adding moxifloxacin (BPaLM) for 24 weeks gave an unfavourable outcome in 11% of patients with rifampin-resistant disease, against 48% on 9–20 months of standard care ([Nyang'wa 2022](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36546625/)).

**Why stacking works here.** Each patient's infection is one organism with one set of targets, so a single drug often works at first. It fails because the pathogen population is large and varied enough to contain a resistant mutant. A second and third drug with independent resistance make escape improbable. The combination answers evolution, not a need for several targets.

### Cancer: driver-defined disease and the first targeted drug

"Durable" here means most patients alive without progression at five years, or survival near the general population's. "Resistance" means median progression-free survival under about three years.

Three patterns stand out.

* **Durable single-drug control is real but narrow.** CML on imatinib comes closest to the infectious-disease picture: one drug, taken for years, with near-normal life expectancy. Lorlatinib in ALK-positive lung cancer has joined it, with most patients progression-free at seven years. Continuous ibrutinib holds most CLL patients for seven years without curing them.
* **The same drug can be durable in one cancer and not another.** Imatinib keeps 83% of CML patients alive at ten years, but in advanced GIST median progression comes at about a year and a half, with a minority surviving ten years. EGFR-driven lung cancer on osimertinib progresses at a median of 19 months even though the drug was built to also inhibit the T790M resistance mutation.
* **Treating earlier may matter.** After surgery for early HER2-positive breast cancer, one year of trastuzumab raised 10-year disease-free survival from 63% to 69% ([Cameron 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28215665/)), while metastatic disease still progresses in most patients despite two HER2 antibodies.

Immunotherapy differs from driver-targeted drugs: when it works it tends to last, but for few. By 2018 an estimated 12.5% of US patients with cancer responded to a checkpoint inhibitor ([Haslam 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31050774/)).

### What the hard-to-treat tail might need

Ordered from strongest to weakest evidence.

**Up-front combinations: randomised Phase 3 evidence, gains measured in months.** In EGFR-mutant lung cancer, adding chemotherapy to osimertinib raised the share alive and progression-free at 24 months from 41% to 57% (hazard ratio 0.62) ([Planchard 2023](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37937763/)). Amivantamab (an EGFR/MET bispecific, one drug on two pathways) with lazertinib beat osimertinib on median progression-free survival (23.7 vs 16.6 months) ([Cho 2024](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38924756/)) and on survival (3-year survival 60% vs 51%), at the cost of more severe side effects (80% vs 52% grade 3 or higher) ([Yang 2025](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40923797/)). In melanoma, adding the MEK inhibitor trametinib to dabrafenib extended median survival from 18.7 to 25.1 months ([Long 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26037941/)). Modelling of tumour evolution predicts that two drugs given together control most tumours if no single mutation confers resistance to both, that large tumours need three, and that simultaneous treatment beats sequential ([Bozic 2013](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23805382/)). None of these combinations has yet turned a resistance cancer into a durable one.

**Most cancer combinations work across patients, not within them.** Patient-to-patient variability with each drug acting independently is enough to explain the benefit of many FDA-approved cancer combinations, with no synergy or additivity: each patient benefits from the drug their tumour is most sensitive to ([Palmer & Sorger 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29245013/)). Of FDA-approved combinations for advanced cancer from 1995 to 2020, 95% had additive or less-than-additive effects on progression-free survival, and additivity predicted every Phase 3 combination from 2014–2018 that improved it ([Hwangbo 2023](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37974028/)). This is the opposite of hepatitis C, HIV and tuberculosis, where every drug in the stack acts on every patient's infection. In the terms of the per-disease labels, cancer combinations mostly reflect "different patients need different targets".

**Immunotherapy: randomised, ten-year evidence of a durable tail, in a minority.** Patients with advanced melanoma who were alive and progression-free three years after nivolumab with or without ipilimumab almost all survived the disease to ten years ([Wolchok 2025](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39282897/)); the open question is how to enlarge that group.

**Personalised neoantigen vaccines: one positive Phase 3, in one setting, short follow-up.** In the Phase 2b KEYNOTE-942 trial (157 patients with resected high-risk melanoma), adding the individualised mRNA vaccine mRNA-4157 (V940) to pembrolizumab lowered recurrence or death (hazard ratio 0.56; p = 0.053) ([Weber 2024](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38246194/)), and the difference held at five years (hazard ratio 0.51; descriptive) ([Khattak 2026](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42223134/)). The Phase 3 INTerpath-001 trial (1,137 patients, stage IIB–IV melanoma after surgery) then raised 24-month recurrence-free survival from 70.0% to 82.2% and distant-metastasis-free survival from 80.2% to 88.5%, with no difference in the first six months; overall survival was not reported and median follow-up was 24.6 months ([Long 2026](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/43013837/)). Every randomised result we found is in melanoma removed by surgery, on top of a checkpoint inhibitor. It says nothing yet about metastatic disease or other cancers.

**Adaptive (evolution-guided) dosing: pilot data only.** Pausing abiraterone in prostate cancer once PSA halved, and restarting when it returned, gave a median time to progression of 33.5 months in 17 men against 14.3 months in 16 contemporaneous men on standard dosing, with less drug ([Zhang 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29180633/); [Zhang 2022](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35762577/)). The comparison was not randomised; the result is a hypothesis.

### Questions for oncology advisors

1. Is "most patients progression-free at five years, or near-normal survival" the right line for durable? Where do GIST on imatinib and CLL on continuous BTK inhibition belong?
2. Lorlatinib leaves 55% of ALK-positive patients progression-free at seven years. Should ALK-driven lung cancer move from resistance to durable, and what separates it from EGFR-driven disease?
3. How should two molecules on one driver or pathway count: ATRA with arsenic in APL, dabrafenib with trametinib in melanoma? As one drug, or as stacking?
4. Do up-front combinations in EGFR-driven lung cancer delay escape within each patient, or treat different patients and subclones? Has any trial been designed to tell these apart?
5. Has any driver-defined cancer been treated with two targeted drugs of non-overlapping resistance from the start, the hepatitis C model, and compared with giving them in sequence?
6. What would it take for neoantigen vaccines to show benefit in metastatic disease, and should the recurrence-free gain in resected melanoma be expected to carry to survival?
