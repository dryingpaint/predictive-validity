Human genetic evidence goes with approval in every area group with enough programs to measure it, clearly so in all but immunology and inflammation, and its effect does not differ measurably between them (p = {{section2.area.tests.genetic_any.p|x2}} for the difference at Phase 2). It is largest in cardiometabolic and endocrine diseases ({{section2.area.rs.cardiometabolic_genetic_any.rs|x2}}×, {{section2.area.rs.cardiometabolic_genetic_any.loa_with|pct0}} against {{section2.area.rs.cardiometabolic_genetic_any.loa_without|pct0}}) and neuroscience ({{section2.area.rs.neuroscience_genetic_any.rs|x2}}×), smaller in oncology ({{section2.area.rs.oncology_genetic_any.rs|x2}}×), and least clear in immunology and inflammation ({{section2.area.rs.immunology_genetic_any.rs|x2}}×, {{section2.area.rs.immunology_genetic_any.rs_lo|x2}}–{{section2.area.rs.immunology_genetic_any.rs_hi|x2}}), where much of the evidence is GWAS. Hematology and the other areas have too few genetically supported programs to estimate. Leaving out any one group keeps the pooled relative success between {{section2.area.loo_min.rs|x2}} and {{section2.area.loo_max|x2}}.

A change in the target's RNA level in diseased tissue is the one line whose effect differs between groups (p = {{section2.area.tests.rna_expression.p|x2}}), going the wrong way in immunology ({{section2.area.rs.immunology_rna_expression.rs|x2}}×); with several lines and groups tested, this is a lead, not a finding.
