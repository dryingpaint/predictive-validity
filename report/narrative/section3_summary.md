Human genetic evidence for the program's own disease is the preclinical evidence that most clearly carried over into Phase 2. Outside oncology, programs whose target had germline genetic evidence for the disease before they entered Phase 2 reached Phase 3 or approval within four years {{section3.h1.g3.rr|x2}} times as often as programs whose target had no germline genetic evidence at all ({{section3.h1.g3.ppv|pct0}} against {{section3.h1.g0.ppv|pct0}}), and the advantage shrinks as the evidence moves to a related disease ({{section3.h1.g2.ppv|pct0}}) or to unrelated diseases only ({{section3.h1.g1.ppv|pct0}}). The same target does better in the diseases its genetics points to than in its other diseases (odds ratio {{section3.h2.or|x2}}), and the effect holds after accounting for an approved drug on the target. The advantage shows in efficacy: genetically supported programs stopped for efficacy less often, though business and operational stops fell about as much. Somatic drivers in oncology and animal models of the disease also predicted advancing, with smaller effects; Reactome pathway evidence pointed the same way without surviving correction, differential expression carried nothing, and a mouse model whose direction matched the drug's did not predict Phase 2 success. Whether the direction of the genetic effect matters could not be tested, because almost no program went against an established mechanism. Grouped by kind, germline genetics, somatic drivers and animal models predict after correcting for the number of kinds tested, and no more specific version of any kind (stronger GWAS, Mendelian evidence, genetics or a mouse model pointing the drug's way) does better than the rest of its kind; an approved drug on the same target for a related disease predicts more strongly still. Genetic support covered only {{section3.coverage.with_g3|pct0}} of the programs that advanced.
