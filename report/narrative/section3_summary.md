Human genetic evidence for the program's own disease is the one kind of preclinical evidence that consistently carried over into Phase 2. Outside oncology, programs whose target had germline genetic evidence for the disease before they entered Phase 2 reached Phase 3 or approval within four years {{section3.h1.g3.rr|x2}} times as often as programs whose target had no germline genetic evidence at all ({{section3.h1.g3.ppv|pct0}} against {{section3.h1.g0.ppv|pct0}}), and the advantage shrinks as the evidence moves to a related disease ({{section3.h1.g2.ppv|pct0}}) or to unrelated diseases only ({{section3.h1.g1.ppv|pct0}}). The same target does better in the diseases its genetics points to than in its other diseases (odds ratio {{section3.h2.or|x2}}), and the effect holds after accounting for an approved drug on the target. The advantage shows in efficacy: genetically supported programs stopped for efficacy less often (business and operational stops fell about as much), and their trials missed the primary endpoint less often on regulatory surrogates and clinician-rated scales; on patient-reported, exploratory-biomarker and event endpoints the difference was small or absent. Somatic drivers in oncology and Reactome pathway evidence carried weaker signals, literature mouse models a marginal one and differential expression none, and a mouse model whose direction matched the drug's did not predict Phase 2 success. Whether the direction of the genetic effect matters could not be tested, because almost no program went against an established mechanism. Of the rules a sponsor could have applied at Phase 2 entry, only the benchmark, an approved drug on the same target for a related disease, met the pre-registered bar for a particularly predictive model; Mendelian evidence for a non-hereditary disease came closest. Genetic support covered only {{section3.coverage.with_g3|pct0}} of the programs that advanced.
