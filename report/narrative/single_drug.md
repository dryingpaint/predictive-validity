## Which diseases the target-based drugging paradigm solves

Most engaged drugs that failed did so because their target did not drive the disease. The other side of that question is how far a right target takes a disease. We asked it of 17 diseases where several approved drugs can be compared on the same yardstick: 12 judged by the share of patients reaching a treatment goal (PASI 90, ACR50, clinical remission, ≥15% weight loss, LDL below 70 mg/dL) and 5 by events prevented (heart failure, coronary artery disease, chronic kidney disease, atrial fibrillation, pulmonary arterial hypertension). Cancer and infection, whose combinations exist to stay ahead of evolution, have their own section.

A disease is **solved by one drug** when one molecule, acting through at most three pathways, gets at least half of patients to the goal beyond placebo, or prevents at least half of events. It is **solved by stacking** when no single drug does but a combination of drugs does, and **unsolved** otherwise. Bispecifics and multi-receptor agonists such as tirzepatide count as one drug.

**In short**

* **Eight of the 17 diseases are solved by one drug, two by stacking, and seven are unsolved.** The count barely moves with the threshold: at 40% it is 9, 3 and 5; at 60%, 8, 1 and 8.
* **The paradigm works best where one pathway carries the disease.** IL-17 blockade (bimekizumab) gets 90% more patients than placebo to PASI 90 in psoriasis; anticoagulation prevents 64% of strokes in atrial fibrillation; PCSK9 blockade gets 68% more patients to the LDL goal.
* **Two of the eight need a drug spanning two pathways.** In obesity and type 2 diabetes the solving drug is tirzepatide (GIP and GLP-1). No single-pathway drug reaches half of patients at ≥15% weight loss.
* **The inflammatory joint and bowel diseases stay unsolved.** The best drug reaches 41% more patients than placebo in rheumatoid arthritis, 38% in ankylosing spondylitis, 35% in Crohn disease and 32% in ulcerative colitis.
* **Where one drug falls short, it is more often different patients than each patient needing several targets.** In rheumatoid arthritis, ankylosing spondylitis and migraine, a drug on a second pathway keeps most of its effect in patients who failed the first. Only ulcerative colitis looks like several targets. Five diseases can't be told apart.
* **Solving the risk factor is not solving the disease.** PCSK9 blockade solves LDL but prevents only 15% of coronary events in a two-year trial, while people born with lower LDL have half the risk per mmol/L. The target is right; the treatment starts decades late.

### Different patients, or several targets?

When one drug helps only some patients, either the rest need a different target or each patient needs more than one. Patients who failed one drug tell them apart: if a drug on a second pathway still works in them, the drugs reach different patients.

* **Rheumatoid arthritis, ankylosing spondylitis and migraine: different patients.** After a TNF blocker fails, drugs on other pathways keep a median 76% of their usual effect in rheumatoid arthritis and 85% in ankylosing spondylitis. The fix is matching patients to targets, not adding targets.
* **Ulcerative colitis: several targets.** After a TNF blocker fails, other drugs keep a median 45% of their effect, and in a randomised trial a co-antibody on two pathways beat one of its components alone.
* **Crohn disease sits between the two** (66% retained). The event diseases can't be told apart, because a hazard ratio doesn't say which patients benefited.

Patients who failed one drug are usually harder to treat, which pushes the retained effect down. So the bias runs against "different patients", and three diseases came out there anyway.

The LDL row is the clearest case of a right target that a drug hits well and that still leaves most of the disease untouched. Genetics says LDL causes coronary disease and that lifelong lowering halves the risk per mmol/L. Two years of near-complete lowering in people who already have disease prevents 15% of events. "Unsolved" here means treated too late as much as "a single target can't do it".

### What this does and doesn't show

The labels rest on published trials and meta-analyses, read against thresholds fixed before any were collected. The thresholds are judgement, and they were set knowing roughly what psoriasis, obesity and rheumatoid arthritis report, so those three labels are partly anticipated; the test lies in the other fourteen. Three labels lean on a single source: psoriatic arthritis (one trial of a drug not approved in the US), pulmonary arterial hypertension (short trials of a drug added to background therapy), and chronic kidney disease (pooled finerenone trials in the stack).
